Daily-life toolkit

CO M M U N I C A T I O N

Talking with someone with dementia

Use short, simple sentences. Make eye contact. Speak slowly. Ask one question at a time. Allow extra time for response. Avoid arguing — redirect instead.

SAFETY

Home safety checklist

Remove throw rugs. Install grab bars. Lock medications. Turn down water heater (≤120°F). Use stove safety knobs. Consider GPS tracker for wandering.

ROUTINE

Build a daily routine

Same wake/sleep times. Bright light + activity in AM. Light dinner; calm evening. Limit naps to 30 min before 3 PM. Familiar music helps transitions.

L E G A L

Essential legal documents

Durable Power of Attorney (financial). Healthcare proxy / advance directive. POLST/MOLST. Will. Complete early while capacity allows. Free state-specific forms at caringinfo.org.

DRIVING

When to stop driving

Warning signs: getting lost in familiar areas, slow reactions, near-misses. AAA Senior Driving has on-road evaluations. Some states have anonymous physician reporting.

M E D I C A T I O N

Medication management

Use pill organizers (weekly). Set alarms or pharmacy auto-text. Brown-bag review with pharmacist annually. Watch for anticholinergic load — see Beers Criteria.

F I N A N C I A L

Financial protection

Consolidate accounts. Set up bill auto-pay. Add a trusted contact at brokerage. Watch for scams (robocalls, romance, grandparent). EverSafe and TrueLink monitor accounts for cognitive-decline patterns.

B E H A V I O R S

Managing difficult moments

Stay calm. Validate emotion ("you seem upset"). Redirect, don't reason. Look for triggers: pain, hunger, full bladder, fatigue, overstimulation. Document patterns to share with clinician.

EATING

Mealtime strategies

Finger foods if utensils difficult. High-contrast plates (red plates increase intake by 25%). Smaller portions, more often. Add nutrition shakes if losing weight.

Caregiver self-assessment

Caregiving for someone with dementia is associated with elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and burnout. Caring for yourself is part of caring for them.

Zarit Burden Interview — sample questions

Score 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always). ≥21 suggests significant burden.

#In the past month…Score
1Do you feel that your relative asks for more help than they need?0–4
2Do you feel you don't have enough time for yourself?0–4
3Do you feel stressed between caregiving and meeting other responsibilities?0–4
4Do you feel embarrassed by your relative's behavior?0–4
5Do you feel angry when around your relative?0–4
6Do you feel your social life has suffered?0–4
7Do you feel you've lost control of your life?0–4

Full 22-item ZBI available from Family Caregiver Alliance.

Warning signs of caregiver burnout

  • Withdrawal from friends and activities you used to enjoy
  • Persistent irritability, sadness, or hopelessness
  • Sleep disturbance not explained by your relative's needs
  • Frequent illness, headaches, GI complaints
  • Increased reliance on alcohol, sleep aids, or other substances
  • Thoughts of self-harm or harming the person you care for

If thoughts of self-harm or harming your relative are present, this is a medical emergency. Call 988 (US Suicide & Crisis Lifeline). You are not alone — these thoughts can occur in caregivers and are treatable.

REACH II evidence base

Multi-component caregiver intervention (REACH II) reduced caregiver depression and improved quality of life across 5 sites.

Components: stress management, problem-solving, support, education, telephone support groups.

Respite is medicine

Adult day programs and respite care reduce caregiver depression scores and delay nursing home placement by an average of 22 months (NYU Caregiver Intervention).
